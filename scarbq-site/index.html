Two zones, not one temperature. Where you set the meat down matters as much as what temp you're running.

Hot zone — closest to the fire Middle zone Cool zone — near the stack
1

Tough, fatty cuts sit closest to the fire. Brisket and pork butt have hours of connective tissue to break down — they can take the heat, and the extra exposure helps them push through the stall.

2

Lean, quick cuts sit near the stack. Chicken, sausage, and wings scorch and dry out fast. Keep them in the cool draw where the heat has already spread out and evened up.

3

Fat cap toward the fire. On a brisket or butt, orient the fat cap so it faces the heat source — it renders down and bastes the meat instead of just dripping off, and it shields the muscle from scorching.

4

Rotate every 1–2 hours. Every smoker runs hotter on the firebox side. Spin your cuts (and swap positions between them) so nothing sits in the hot spot the whole cook.

5

A water pan between fire and meat buffers temperature spikes and keeps the cool zone from drying out — put it in the middle of the chamber, not right under any one cut.

6

Probe the thickest part, away from bone and fat. Bone reads hotter, fat reads slower — either one will lie to you about doneness.

The wood is half the flavor. Go light with strong ones and heavier with mild ones — you can always add more smoke, you can't take it away.

After the Cook: Storage & Reheat

Cooling down: Don't let smoked meat sit out past 2 hours (1 hour if it's above 90°F outside). Slice or pull what you're serving now, and get the rest into the fridge or a cooler with ice.
Fridge (3–4 days): Wrap tight in foil or a vacuum bag, add a splash of leftover juice or broth before sealing — it dries out fast once it's off the smoker.
Freezer (2–3 months): Vacuum seal if you can. Portion it first — you'll want to thaw one meal at a time, not the whole brisket.
Reheating low and slow: Wrap in foil with a splash of broth, oven at 250°F until it hits about 165°F internal. Slow is what keeps it from drying out — resist the microwave for anything sliced.
Reheating fast: Sauce-covered or saucy cuts (pulled pork, chopped brisket) hold up fine in the microwave, covered, in short bursts. Bark-forward cuts like ribs or sliced brisket do better finished under a broiler or back on the smoker for 15–20 min.

How competition judges actually score a plate — plus a version for grading your own backyard cook.

Competition scoring, in short (KCBS-style): each turn-in box gets three scores of 1–9 from every judge on the table — Appearance, Taste, and Tenderness — and taste usually counts double. A 9 means a judge would eat every bite in front of you; a 2 means something's actually wrong with it. Most solid boxes land in the 6–8 range — save 9s for the one that stops the table.

Appearance

7/10

Color, glaze or bark, smoke ring, how clean the cut looks before anyone tastes it.

Taste

7/10

Smoke, seasoning, and sauce (if any) working together — nothing should be shouting over the meat.

Tenderness

7/10

Pulls or bites apart clean — not mushy, not fighting back.
